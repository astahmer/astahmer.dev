## Design Context

### Users

Primary audience: OSS peers who already know Alex through packages, GitHub work, articles, or technical discussions and
want a fast signal of depth and taste.

Secondary audience: prospective clients and recruiters evaluating technical credibility, communication style, and
whether Alex feels like someone they can trust with complex frontend, backend or tooling work.

Tertiary audience: conference organizers and adjacent industry peers looking for someone with clear opinions, strong
technical range, and enough writing quality to suggest talk potential.

Most visitors will arrive from a package page, GitHub profile, social post, shared article, or search result. They
should be able to quickly understand what Alex is good at, browse standout work, and settle into long-form reading
comfortably.

Core jobs to be done:

- Establish technical credibility quickly.
- Showcase meaningful open source and technical project work.
- Support readable long-form browsing for 10 minutes or more.
- Grow readership without turning the site into a publication product.
- Create enough trust and clarity that hiring, collaboration, or speaking opportunities feel natural.

### Brand Personality

Keep the current voice: informal, technically opinionated, and human.

Three guiding personality words:

- Opinionated
- Trustworthy
- Curious

Emotional goals:

- Spark curiosity fast.
- Build trust through clarity and restraint.
- Leave a light sense of delight without becoming flashy or self-conscious.

### Aesthetic Direction

Direction: quiet editorial minimalism with a slight paper-like, organic brutalist edge.

The site should feel visually pleasing, calm, and highly readable first, with only a slight technical undertone. It
should not look like a developer dashboard, documentation theme, hacker blog, or startup landing page. The visual
language should borrow from the straightforward readability and simplicity of Overreacted, Evan You, Antfu, the
paper-like feel of npmx.dev, and the easy reading rhythm of Joe Basser's writing.

The site should explicitly avoid:

- Loud, overly art-directed personal-brand visuals.
- Generic blog aesthetics that feel basic or templated.
- Overly tech-coded cues such as code-editor vibes, nerdy type choices, or aggressive developer-brand signaling.
- Overwhelming scale, too much contrast, or animations that distract from reading.

Theme strategy: light and dark mode are both first-class experiences. Neither mode should feel like an afterthought.
Both should stay soft on the eyes, low-glare, and comfortable for extended reading and scrolling.

Color direction: restrained, low-intensity palette with tinted neutrals and subtle accents. Avoid strong or overly bold
colors.

Motion direction: none or minimal. Any movement should be quiet, functional, and never compete with reading.

### Design Principles

1. Reading comes first. Every layout, spacing, color, and type decision should improve scanability and long-form
   comfort.
2. Credibility over performance. The site should signal depth and taste through clarity, curation, and writing quality
   rather than visual spectacle.
3. Technical, not nerdy. Keep a slight technical undertone, but avoid code-blog clichés, terminal aesthetics, and heavy
   developer theming.
4. Quiet personality beats loud branding. Preserve Alex's opinionated, human voice through copy and subtle details
   instead of flashy decoration.
5. Minimal does not mean generic. The design should stay restrained while still feeling intentional, memorable, and
   distinct from common portfolio/blog templates.
6. Both themes must feel native. Light and dark mode should each be carefully tuned for comfort, readability, and tonal
   consistency.
